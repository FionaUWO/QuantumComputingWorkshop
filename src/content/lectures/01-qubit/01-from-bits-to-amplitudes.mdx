Flip a fair coin and you get a random bit. Flip it again and you still have a random bit. No amount of further flipping makes the outcome any easier to predict.

A qubit behaves differently. There is a gate, written $H$, that takes a qubit in a definite state and makes its measurement outcome a fair coin flip. Apply $H$ a second time and the outcome is certain again.

Run both before reading on. The first gives roughly fifty of each outcome. The second gives $0$ a hundred times out of a hundred.

Where did the randomness go? This lecture is the answer, and it takes six sections to give it. We begin by checking that ordinary probability cannot produce this behaviour.

### A random bit

A classical bit is an element of $\{0,1\}$. If we are unsure which, we describe it by a probability vector

$$
p = \begin{pmatrix} p_0 \\ p_1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_0, p_1 \ge 0, \quad p_0 + p_1 = 1.
$$

An operation on a random bit is a linear map that sends probability vectors to probability vectors. Applying it to the two basis vectors shows what such a matrix must look like: each column is itself a probability vector. These are the *stochastic matrices*. Two examples are the NOT gate and a noisy channel that flips the bit with probability $\varepsilon$:

$$
\mathrm{NOT} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
N_\varepsilon = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\varepsilon & \varepsilon \\ \varepsilon & 1-\varepsilon \end{pmatrix}.
$$

So the theory of a random bit is: vectors with non-negative entries and unit $1$-norm, $\lVert p \rVert_1 = \sum_i |p_i| = 1$, acted on by the linear maps that preserve those two properties.

No such map can play the part of $H$. Suppose a stochastic matrix $M$ turns the bit $0$ into a fair coin, $M e_0 = \tfrac12 e_0 + \tfrac12 e_1$. Applying it again,

$$
M^2 e_0 = \tfrac12\, M e_0 + \tfrac12\, M e_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac14 \\[0.2em] \tfrac14 \end{pmatrix} + \tfrac12\, M e_1 .
$$

The entries of $M e_1$ are non-negative, so outcome $1$ keeps probability at least $\tfrac14$. Certainty does not come back, because nothing in a probability vector can cancel.

### Two changes

Quantum theory changes two things. The entries become complex numbers, called *amplitudes*, and the norm that is preserved is the Euclidean $2$-norm. The whole lecture follows from that swap:

| | Random bit | Qubit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State | $p \in \mathbb{R}^2$, $p_i \ge 0$, $\lVert p \rVert_1 = 1$ | $\psi \in \mathbb{C}^2$, $\lVert \psi \rVert_2 = 1$ |
| Operations | stochastic matrices | unitary matrices |
| Reading it out | look at the bit | measure, with probabilities $\lvert\psi_i\rvert^2$ |

The first change is the one that matters for the two buttons above: an amplitude can be negative, and a negative number can cancel a positive one. The next four sections set up what we need to follow that cancellation, namely states, measurement, a picture of a qubit, and gates. Section 6 then returns to the experiment.
