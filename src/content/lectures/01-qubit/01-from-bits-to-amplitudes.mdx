Flip a fair coin and you get a random bit. Flip it again and you still have a random bit. No amount of further flipping makes the outcome any easier to predict.

A qubit behaves differently. There is a gate, written $H$, that takes a qubit in a definite state and makes its measurement outcome a fair coin flip. Apply $H$ a second time and the outcome is certain again.

Run both before reading on. The first gives roughly fifty of each outcome. The second gives $0$ a hundred times out of a hundred.

Where did the randomness go? This lecture is the answer, and it takes six sections to give it. We begin by checking that ordinary probability cannot produce this behaviour.

### A random bit

A classical bit is either $0$ or $1$. If we are unsure which, we describe it by two numbers: the probability $p_0$ that it is $0$ and the probability $p_1$ that it is $1$. Written as a column they form a *probability vector*

$$
p = \begin{pmatrix} p_0 \\ p_1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_0, p_1 \ge 0, \quad p_0 + p_1 = 1.
$$

Two of these vectors describe a bit we are sure about, and we give them names:

$$
e_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \;\text{ (certainly 0)}, \qquad
e_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \;\text{ (certainly 1)}.
$$

Every probability vector is a mixture of the two, $p = p_0\,e_0 + p_1\,e_1$. A fair coin, for instance, is $\tfrac12 e_0 + \tfrac12 e_1$.

<Example>
A bit that is $0$ seven times in ten is

$$
p = \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 \\ 0.3 \end{pmatrix} = 0.7\,e_0 + 0.3\,e_1 .
$$

Both entries are non-negative and $0.7 + 0.3 = 1$. By contrast $(0.7,\ 0.5)$ is not a probability vector, because its entries sum to $1.2$, and neither is $(1.2,\ -0.2)$, because a probability cannot be negative.
</Example>

### Operations on a random bit

An operation on a random bit is a $2 \times 2$ matrix $M$ that sends probability vectors to probability vectors. Read such a matrix one column at a time:

- The **first column** is $M e_0$: what the operation does to a bit that is certainly $0$.
- The **second column** is $M e_1$: what it does to a bit that is certainly $1$.
- Within a column, the top entry is the probability that the output is $0$ and the bottom entry the probability that it is $1$.

So each column is itself a probability vector: its entries are non-negative and sum to $1$. Matrices of this kind are called *stochastic matrices*.

The NOT gate flips the bit. An input $0$ certainly becomes $1$, so the first column is $(0, 1)$. An input $1$ certainly becomes $0$, so the second column is $(1, 0)$.

<Example>
Apply NOT to the bit that is $0$ seven times in ten:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0.7 \\ 0.3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.3 \\ 0.7 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The two probabilities trade places: the bit is now $1$ seven times in ten.
</Example>

A noisy channel $N_\varepsilon$ flips the bit with probability $\varepsilon$ and leaves it alone otherwise. Here $\varepsilon$ is the Greek letter epsilon, a number between $0$ and $1$ that is usually small. An input $0$ stays $0$ with probability $1 - \varepsilon$ and becomes $1$ with probability $\varepsilon$, so the first column is $(1-\varepsilon,\ \varepsilon)$. The second column follows in the same way. Side by side:

$$
\mathrm{NOT} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
N_\varepsilon = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\varepsilon & \varepsilon \\ \varepsilon & 1-\varepsilon \end{pmatrix}.
$$

<Example>
Take $\varepsilon = 0.1$, a channel that gets one bit in ten wrong, and send through it a bit that is $0$ with probability $0.7$:

$$
N_{0.1}\begin{pmatrix} 0.7 \\ 0.3 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 \times 0.7 + 0.1 \times 0.3 \\ 0.1 \times 0.7 + 0.9 \times 0.3 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0.66 \\ 0.34 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The top entry adds up the two ways of ending at $0$: start at $0$ and not be flipped, or start at $1$ and be flipped. The result is again a probability vector.
</Example>

<Example>
"Throw the bit away and flip a fair coin" is also an operation. Whatever the input, the output is $0$ or $1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each, so both columns are $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$:

$$
F = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\[0.2em] \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
F\begin{pmatrix} 0.7 \\ 0.3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.35 + 0.15 \\ 0.35 + 0.15 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Applying $F$ to the result changes nothing: a fair coin flipped again is still a fair coin. This is the first sentence of the lecture written as a matrix.
</Example>

### Why no coin can do what H does

Suppose some stochastic matrix $M$ turns a bit that is certainly $0$ into a fair coin:

$$
M e_0 = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 \\[0.2em] \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix} = \tfrac12 e_0 + \tfrac12 e_1 .
$$

Apply $M$ a second time. The bit is now $0$ half the time and $1$ half the time, and $M$ acts on each case separately:

$$
M^2 e_0 = M\big(\tfrac12 e_0 + \tfrac12 e_1\big) = \tfrac12\, M e_0 + \tfrac12\, M e_1 .
$$

The first term we know: $\tfrac12\, M e_0$ is half of a fair coin, the vector $(\tfrac14, \tfrac14)$. The second term we do not know, but $M e_1$ is a probability vector, so its entries cannot be negative. Therefore

$$
M^2 e_0 = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac14 \\[0.2em] \tfrac14 \end{pmatrix} + \tfrac12\, M e_1
\qquad\text{has bottom entry at least } \tfrac14 .
$$

The bottom entry is the probability of outcome $1$. In words: half the time the bit is $0$ before the second step, and from there it lands on $1$ half the time. Whatever $M$ does in the other case can only add to that quarter. Certainty does not come back, because probabilities add and never cancel.

### Two changes

Quantum theory keeps the same picture, a column of two numbers acted on by matrices. It changes two rules.

**The entries may be negative, or complex.** They are no longer probabilities. They are called *amplitudes*, and an amplitude can be any complex number. For example $\tfrac12,\ -\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac{i}{2}$ are all allowed.

**The squares must total one.** The entries of a probability vector sum to one. For a vector of amplitudes $\psi = (\psi_0, \psi_1)$ it is the squared sizes that sum to one:

$$
|\psi_0|^2 + |\psi_1|^2 = 1 .
$$

Here $|\psi_i|$ is the absolute value of a real number, or the modulus of a complex one. In geometric terms the vector has length $1$.

<Example>
The fair coin is the probability vector $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, because its entries sum to one. Two vectors of amplitudes with entries of equal size are

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \\[0.3em] \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \end{pmatrix}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \\[0.3em] -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \end{pmatrix},
$$

both allowed because $\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big)^2 + \big(\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big)^2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$. The second has no counterpart among probability vectors.
</Example>

<Example>
Three quick checks of the rule.

- $(\tfrac35,\ \tfrac45)$ is allowed: $\tfrac{9}{25} + \tfrac{16}{25} = 1$. So is $(\tfrac35,\ -\tfrac45)$, since squaring removes the sign.
- $(\tfrac12,\ \tfrac12)$ is a probability vector but not a vector of amplitudes: $\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$, not $1$.
- $(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\ \tfrac{i}{\sqrt2})$ is allowed. The modulus of $\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}$ is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, so the squared sizes are again $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12$.
</Example>

Amplitudes are connected to what we observe by one more rule: the squared size of each amplitude is the probability of that outcome. Both vectors in the example give probabilities $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac12$, so each looks exactly like a fair coin when measured. That is the first button. Likewise $(\tfrac35, \tfrac45)$ and $(\tfrac35, -\tfrac45)$ both give outcome $0$ with probability $0.36$ and outcome $1$ with probability $0.64$. The vectors in each pair are still different: they differ by a sign that this measurement cannot see. A second $H$ can see it, and that is what the second button relies on.

Side by side:

| | Random bit | Qubit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State | two probabilities: non-negative, summing to $1$ | two amplitudes: complex, squared sizes summing to $1$ |
| Reading it out | look at the bit | measure; outcome $i$ has probability $\lvert\psi_i\rvert^2$ |
| Operations | stochastic matrices, which keep the sum equal to $1$ | *unitary* matrices, which keep the length equal to $1$ |

Sections 1.2, 1.3 and 1.5 take up the three rows in turn.

Mathematicians have names for the two ways of totalling. The sum of absolute values $|p_0| + |p_1|$ is the *$1$-norm* of a vector and the length $\sqrt{|\psi_0|^2 + |\psi_1|^2}$ is its *$2$-norm*. In that language the whole change is small: complex entries are allowed, and one norm is swapped for the other.

The first change is the one that matters for the two buttons: an amplitude can be negative, and a negative number can cancel a positive one. The next four sections set up what we need to follow that cancellation, namely states, measurement, a picture of a qubit, and gates. Section 1.6 then returns to the experiment.
