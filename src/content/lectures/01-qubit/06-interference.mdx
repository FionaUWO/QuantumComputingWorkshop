We return to the two buttons of Section 1.1: one $H$ gives a coin flip, two give certainty.

The states $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ differ only in a relative phase, and a computational-basis measurement cannot tell them apart: both give $0$ and $1$ with probability $\tfrac12$. A coin flip would do the same.

But apply $H$ before measuring. Since $H|+\rangle = |0\rangle$ and $H|-\rangle = |1\rangle$, the outcome is now certain and distinguishes the two states perfectly. Follow the amplitudes through two Hadamards applied to $|0\rangle$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
|0\rangle \;&\xrightarrow{\;H\;}\; \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|1\rangle \\[0.4em]
&\xrightarrow{\;H\;}\; \tfrac12\big(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\big) + \tfrac12\big(|0\rangle - |1\rangle\big) = |0\rangle .
\end{aligned}
$$

There are two paths to the outcome $1$, through $|0\rangle$ and through $|1\rangle$, with amplitudes $+\tfrac12$ and $-\tfrac12$. They cancel. The two paths to $0$ both have amplitude $+\tfrac12$ and add to $1$.

That is where the randomness went. Nothing was lost after one $H$: the state $|+\rangle$ only looks like a coin flip to a measurement, and it is a different state from $|-\rangle$, which looks the same. The second $H$ tells them apart. It can do so because one of its own entries is negative, so the two contributions to outcome $1$ cancel.

Now do the same with probabilities. Section 1.1 showed that no stochastic matrix can restore certainty. For the fair coin flip itself the matrix is

$$
F = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad F^2 = F .
$$

Flipping twice is the same as flipping once, because the entries are non-negative and nothing can cancel. Probabilities add; amplitudes can cancel. This is *interference*, and every quantum algorithm in the coming lectures is an arrangement for wrong answers to cancel.

### Dialling the phase

Put a phase gate $P(\varphi) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\varphi} \end{pmatrix}$ between the two Hadamards. Then

$$
\begin{gathered}
H\,P(\varphi)\,H\,|0\rangle = \frac{1 + e^{i\varphi}}{2}\,|0\rangle + \frac{1 - e^{i\varphi}}{2}\,|1\rangle, \\[0.6em]
P(0) = \Big|\frac{1 + e^{i\varphi}}{2}\Big|^2 = \cos^2\frac\varphi2 .
\end{gathered}
$$

The relative phase, invisible to a direct measurement, has been converted into a probability. This three-gate circuit is an interferometer, and it is the seed of phase estimation in lecture 4. The gates $Z$, $S$ and $T$ are $P(\pi)$, $P(\tfrac\pi2)$ and $P(\tfrac\pi4)$.

| Middle gate | $\varphi$ | $P(0) = \cos^2\tfrac\varphi2$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| none | $0$ | $1$ |
| $T$ | $\pi/4$ | $\tfrac12\big(1 + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big) \approx 0.854$ |
| $S$ | $\pi/2$ | $\tfrac12$ |
| $Z$ | $\pi$ | $0$ |

### What comes next

Everything in this lecture fits on one sphere. A single qubit is a point on it, a gate is a rotation, and a classical computer can keep track of both with two angles. If that were the whole story, quantum computing would offer nothing new.

Two qubits are not two points on two spheres. Most states of a pair cannot be described one qubit at a time, and the number of amplitudes doubles with every qubit added. Lecture 2 starts there.
