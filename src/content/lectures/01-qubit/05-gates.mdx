The states a computational measurement cannot separate lie on the equator of the sphere. To get at them we have to move the state before measuring, so we need to know which moves are allowed.

An operation on a qubit must be linear, and it must send unit vectors to unit vectors.

<Statement>
**Proposition.** A linear map $U$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$ satisfies $\lVert U\psi\rVert = \lVert\psi\rVert$ for every $\psi$ if and only if $U^\dagger U = I$. Such matrices are called *unitary*.
</Statement>

*Proof.* We have $\lVert U\psi\rVert^2 = \langle\psi|U^\dagger U|\psi\rangle$, so if $U^\dagger U = I$ the norm is preserved. Conversely, put $A = U^\dagger U - I$. Then $A$ is Hermitian and $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle = 0$ for all $\psi$. Taking $\psi$ to be an eigenvector shows every eigenvalue of $A$ is zero, and a Hermitian matrix with only zero eigenvalues is zero. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Equivalently, the columns of $U$ form an orthonormal basis, and $U$ preserves all inner products: $\langle U\phi|U\psi\rangle = \langle\phi|\psi\rangle$. Every unitary is invertible, with $U^{-1} = U^\dagger$, so quantum gates never lose information; only measurement does. Compare the noisy channel $N_{1/2}$ of the first section, which sends every probability vector to $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ and cannot be undone.

| Gate | Matrix | On the sphere |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $X$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | half turn about the $x$-axis |
| $Y$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | half turn about the $y$-axis |
| $Z$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ | half turn about the $z$-axis |
| $H$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ | half turn about the axis midway between $x$ and $z$ |
| $S$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}$ | quarter turn about the $z$-axis |
| $T$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\pi/4} \end{pmatrix}$ | eighth turn about the $z$-axis |

$X$, $Y$ and $Z$ are the Pauli matrices again, now used as gates. $S$ is called the *phase gate* and $T$ the *$\pi/8$ gate*; the odd name comes from writing $T = e^{i\pi/8}\begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\pi/8} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\pi/8} \end{pmatrix}$. They are related by $T^2 = S$ and $S^2 = Z$, and $H = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(X + Z)$.

A sequence of gates is drawn as a *circuit*, a wire read from left to right:

$$
|0\rangle \;\text{---}\; \boxed{H} \;\text{---}\; \boxed{Z} \;\text{---}\; \boxed{H} \;\text{---}
$$

The matrix product is written the other way round, because the first gate applied stands next to the state: the circuit "$A$, then $B$" is the matrix $BA$. For the palindrome $HZH$ the order makes no difference, but for "$H$, then $T$" the matrix is $TH$.

The Hadamard gate $H$ is the change of basis between $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ and $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$: its columns are $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$, so $H|0\rangle = |+\rangle$, $H|1\rangle = |-\rangle$, and $H^2 = I$ sends them back. Conjugating by $H$ therefore exchanges the roles of the $x$- and $z$-axes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
HZH &= \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \\[0.4em]
&= \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = X .
\end{aligned}
$$

### Every gate is a rotation

The third column of the table is no accident. Let $\hat n$ be a unit vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The relations between the Pauli matrices give $(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)^2 = I$, so the exponential series splits into even and odd powers exactly as it does for $e^{i\theta}$:

$$
R_{\hat n}(\theta) := e^{-i\theta\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma/2} = \cos\tfrac\theta2\, I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma).
$$

<Statement>
**Theorem.** $R_{\hat n}(\theta)$ is unitary with determinant $1$, and it moves the Bloch vector of every state by a rotation through the angle $\theta$ about the axis $\hat n$. Every $2 \times 2$ unitary has the form $e^{i\delta} R_{\hat n}(\theta)$.
</Statement>

We check one case and leave the general statement to the exercises. About the $z$-axis,

$$
\begin{gathered}
R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\theta/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\theta/2} \end{pmatrix}, \\[0.6em]
R_z(\theta)\begin{pmatrix} \cos\tfrac{\vartheta}{2} \\ e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\vartheta}{2} \end{pmatrix}
= e^{-i\theta/2}\begin{pmatrix} \cos\tfrac{\vartheta}{2} \\ e^{i(\varphi+\theta)}\sin\tfrac{\vartheta}{2} \end{pmatrix},
\end{gathered}
$$

which, after discarding the global phase, is the same polar angle with azimuth $\varphi + \theta$. The gates in the table are rotations up to a global phase:

$$
\begin{gathered}
X = i\,R_x(\pi), \qquad Z = i\,R_z(\pi), \\[0.5em]
H = i\,R_{\hat h}(\pi) \;\text{ with }\; \hat h = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\hat x + \hat z), \\[0.5em]
S = e^{i\pi/4} R_z\big(\tfrac\pi2\big), \qquad T = e^{i\pi/8} R_z\big(\tfrac\pi4\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

### Building gates from rotations

Rotations about two axes are enough to reach every gate.

<Statement>
**Theorem (Z-Y decomposition).** For every $2 \times 2$ unitary $U$ there are real numbers $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ with

$$
U = e^{i\alpha}\, R_z(\beta)\, R_y(\gamma)\, R_z(\delta).
$$
</Statement>

This is the matrix form of Euler angles: any rotation of the sphere is a turn about $z$, then about $y$, then about $z$ again. For example, since $R_y(\tfrac\pi2) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $R_x(\pi) = -iX$,

$$
H = i\, R_x(\pi)\, R_y\big(\tfrac\pi2\big),
$$

a quarter turn about the $y$-axis followed by a half turn about the $x$-axis. Try it on the sphere with your hand: the result is the same as the single half turn about the diagonal axis.

A real device cannot implement a continuum of angles exactly. It does not need to: products of $H$ and $T$ alone come arbitrarily close to any single-qubit gate, up to global phase. We will use this fact without proof.

### Aside: the group behind the sphere

In the language of groups: the matrices $R_{\hat n}(\theta)$ make up $SU(2)$, and the theorem describes a surjective homomorphism $SU(2) \to SO(3)$. It is two-to-one, since $R_{\hat n}(2\pi) = -I$: a full turn of the sphere is the identity rotation but the matrix $-I$, which differs from $I$ by a global phase only. Topologically $SU(2) \cong S^3$ is the double cover of $SO(3)$.

### Back to the two buttons

Half of the puzzle is now solved by geometry. $H$ is a half turn about the axis midway between $x$ and $z$. It carries the north pole $|0\rangle$ to the point $|+\rangle$ on the equator, where the outcome is a coin flip, and a second half turn about the same axis carries it home. What the sphere does not show is why a coin cannot make the same round trip. For that we follow the amplitudes themselves, in the next section.
