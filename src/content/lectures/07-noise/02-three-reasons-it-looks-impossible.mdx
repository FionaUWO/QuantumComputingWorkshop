Classical information is protected by redundancy. The *repetition code* stores each bit three times,

$$
0 \mapsto 000, \qquad 1 \mapsto 111 ,
$$

and decodes by majority vote. If each bit flips independently with probability $p$, the vote fails only when two or three bits flip, with probability $3p^2(1-p) + p^3 = 3p^2 - 2p^3$. That is less than $p$ whenever $p \lt \tfrac12$.

</Figure>

Three obstacles seem to block the quantum version.

1. **No cloning.** We cannot store $|\psi\rangle$ three times as $|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$; lecture 2 proved that no unitary does this.
2. **Measurement destroys.** A majority vote requires looking at the bits. Measuring a qubit collapses the superposition we are trying to protect.
3. **Errors are continuous.** A bit is either flipped or not. A qubit can be off by any small rotation, and identifying the exact error would seem to need infinite precision.

Each of these has an answer, and all three answers are already visible in the smallest example.
