Every algorithm so far assumed perfect gates on perfectly isolated qubits. Real qubits interact with their surroundings and real gates are slightly off. The estimate at the end of lecture 6 showed how little it takes: an error rate of one in a thousand per gate limits a computation to a few hundred gates. Shor's algorithm for numbers of cryptographic size needs billions.

### Pauli errors

The simplest models say that an unwanted Pauli matrix is applied at random.

- **Bit flip.** With probability $p$ the qubit suffers $X$, which exchanges $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. Otherwise nothing happens.
- **Phase flip.** With probability $p$ the qubit suffers $Z$, which sends $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ to $\alpha|0\rangle - \beta|1\rangle$. This error has no classical counterpart: it does nothing to a bit, and it exchanges $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$.
- **Depolarising noise.** With probability $p$ an error occurs, and it is $X$, $Y$ or $Z$ with probability $\tfrac p3$ each. Since $Y = iXZ$, a $Y$ error is a bit flip and a phase flip together.

On the Bloch sphere the bit flip channel shrinks the sphere towards the $x$-axis, the phase flip channel towards the $z$-axis, and the depolarising channel towards the centre. The result of such a process is a probabilistic mixture of states, which needs the language of density matrices to describe fully; we will only need to track which Pauli was applied.

### Errors that are not Pauli matrices

Physical errors are rarely exact flips. A gate that over-rotates by a small angle $\epsilon$ applies

$$
e^{-i\epsilon X} = \cos\epsilon\; I - i\sin\epsilon\; X ,
$$

and there is a continuum of such errors. It will turn out, remarkably, that a code able to correct $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ corrects all of them.
