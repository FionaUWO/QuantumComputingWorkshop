## From bits to amplitudes

A classical bit is an element of $\{0,1\}$. If we are unsure which, we describe it by a probability vector

$$
p = \begin{pmatrix} p_0 \\ p_1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p_0, p_1 \ge 0, \quad p_0 + p_1 = 1.
$$

An operation on a random bit is a linear map that sends probability vectors to probability vectors. Applying it to the two basis vectors shows what such a matrix must look like: each column is itself a probability vector. These are the *stochastic matrices*. Two examples are the NOT gate and a noisy channel that flips the bit with probability $\varepsilon$:

$$
\mathrm{NOT} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
N_\varepsilon = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\varepsilon & \varepsilon \\ \varepsilon & 1-\varepsilon \end{pmatrix}.
$$

So the theory of a random bit is: vectors with non-negative entries and unit $1$-norm, $\lVert p \rVert_1 = \sum_i |p_i| = 1$, acted on by the linear maps that preserve those two properties.

Quantum theory changes two things. The entries become complex numbers, called *amplitudes*, and the norm that is preserved is the Euclidean $2$-norm. The whole lecture follows from that swap:

| | Random bit | Qubit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State | $p \in \mathbb{R}^2$, $p_i \ge 0$, $\lVert p \rVert_1 = 1$ | $\psi \in \mathbb{C}^2$, $\lVert \psi \rVert_2 = 1$ |
| Operations | stochastic matrices | unitary matrices |
| Reading it out | look at the bit | measure, with probabilities $\lvert\psi_i\rvert^2$ |

## States

<Statement>
**Definition.** A state of a qubit is a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{C}, \quad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1.
$$
</Statement>

The symbol $|\psi\rangle$ is read "ket psi" and simply means a column vector. The standard basis vectors get the names

$$
|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

so every state can be written $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$. A state with both amplitudes non-zero is called a *superposition* of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$.

### Inner products

The "bra" $\langle\psi|$ is the conjugate transpose of $|\psi\rangle$, a row vector: $\langle\psi| = (\bar\alpha \;\; \bar\beta)$. A bra times a ket is the Hermitian inner product on $\mathbb{C}^2$. For $|\phi\rangle = \gamma|0\rangle + \delta|1\rangle$,

$$
\begin{gathered}
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \bar\gamma\,\alpha + \bar\delta\,\beta, \qquad
\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \overline{\langle\phi|\psi\rangle}, \\[0.5em]
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = \lVert\psi\rVert^2 .
\end{gathered}
$$

It is linear in the ket and conjugate-linear in the bra. The normalisation condition reads $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1$, and two states are *orthogonal* when $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = 0$.

<Example>
**Example.** The vector $(1,\; 1+i)$ has squared norm $|1|^2 + |1+i|^2 = 1 + 2 = 3$, so

$$
|\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\,|0\rangle + \tfrac{1+i}{\sqrt3}\,|1\rangle
$$

is a state. We will carry it through the lecture. Since $1 + i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$, its amplitudes have moduli $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$ and $\sqrt{\tfrac23}$, and the second is rotated by $\pi/4$ relative to the first.
</Example>

### Other bases

Four superpositions come up constantly:

$$
|\pm\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle \pm |1\rangle\big), \qquad |{\pm i}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle \pm i|1\rangle\big).
$$

A direct computation gives $\langle +|-\rangle = \tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0$ and $\langle +|+\rangle = \langle -|-\rangle = 1$, so $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{C}^2$, just as good as the standard one. The same holds for $\{|{+i}\rangle, |{-i}\rangle\}$. In any orthonormal basis $\{|b_0\rangle, |b_1\rangle\}$ the coordinates of a state are inner products:

$$
\begin{gathered}
|\psi\rangle = \langle b_0|\psi\rangle\,|b_0\rangle + \langle b_1|\psi\rangle\,|b_1\rangle, \\[0.5em]
|\langle b_0|\psi\rangle|^2 + |\langle b_1|\psi\rangle|^2 = 1 .
\end{gathered}
$$

The second identity is Parseval's: the norm of a vector does not depend on the orthonormal basis used to compute it.

## Measurement

We cannot read $\alpha$ and $\beta$ off a qubit. The only way to get information out is to measure it, and a measurement returns a single classical bit.

<Statement>
**Born rule.** Measuring $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ in the standard basis gives outcome $0$ with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and outcome $1$ with probability $|\beta|^2$. Afterwards the state is $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$, matching the outcome.
</Statement>

The normalisation condition is exactly what makes these probabilities sum to one. Measurement is destructive: one copy of a state yields one bit, and the amplitudes are gone.

### Measuring in another basis

Nothing singles out the standard basis. For any orthonormal basis $\{|b_0\rangle, |b_1\rangle\}$ there is a measurement with

$$
P(i) = |\langle b_i|\psi\rangle|^2, \qquad \text{state afterwards: } |b_i\rangle .
$$

By Parseval these are non-negative and sum to one, so the rule is consistent. The standard-basis rule is the special case $\langle 0|\psi\rangle = \alpha$, $\langle 1|\psi\rangle = \beta$.

<Example>
**Example.** Take $|\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\big(|0\rangle + (1+i)|1\rangle\big)$ from before. In the standard basis, $P(0) = \tfrac13$ and $P(1) = \tfrac23$. In the basis $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle +|\psi\rangle &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}\big(1 + (1+i)\big) = \tfrac{2+i}{\sqrt6}, \\[0.4em]
\langle -|\psi\rangle &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}\big(1 - (1+i)\big) = \tfrac{-i}{\sqrt6},
\end{aligned}
$$

so $P(+) = \tfrac56$ and $P(-) = \tfrac16$. The phase of $\beta$ played no part in the first measurement and decided the second.
</Example>

### How many copies?

To estimate $p = |\alpha|^2$ you need many identically prepared copies. Measuring $N$ of them gives independent bits, and the number $k$ of zeros is binomial. The estimate $\hat p = k/N$ satisfies

$$
\mathbb{E}[\hat p] = p, \qquad \operatorname{sd}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}} \;\le\; \frac{1}{2\sqrt N} .
$$

With $N = 100$ the standard deviation can be as large as $0.05$, so a count of $44$ or $57$ out of $100$ is entirely consistent with $p = \tfrac12$. Each further decimal digit of precision costs a factor of $100$ in copies.

## The Bloch sphere

### Global phase

For any real $\gamma$ and any basis vector $|b\rangle$,

$$
\big|\langle b|\,e^{i\gamma}\psi\rangle\big|^2 = |e^{i\gamma}|^2\,|\langle b|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\langle b|\psi\rangle|^2 .
$$

So $|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$ give identical probabilities for every measurement, and they describe the same physical state. The factor $e^{i\gamma}$ is called a *global phase*. A *relative* phase, as in $|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle$, is a different matter: the example above shows it changes probabilities.

Count real parameters. $\mathbb{C}^2$ has four; normalisation removes one, leaving the sphere $S^3$; global phase removes another. A qubit state is a point of a two-dimensional space, namely the complex projective line $\mathbb{CP}^1 = S^3/U(1)$, and $\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2$.

### Coordinates

Use the phase freedom to make $\alpha$ real and non-negative. Then $\alpha = \cos\tfrac\theta2$ for a unique $\theta \in [0, \pi]$, and $|\beta| = \sin\tfrac\theta2$, so every state has the form

$$
\begin{gathered}
|\psi\rangle = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle, \\[0.4em]
0 \le \theta \le \pi, \quad 0 \le \varphi \lt 2\pi .
\end{gathered}
$$

We plot it at the point of the unit sphere with polar angle $\theta$ and azimuth $\varphi$:

$$
\vec n = (x, y, z) = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\; \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\; \cos\theta).
$$

This is the *Bloch vector* of the state. It can be computed without first fixing the phase, because $2\bar\alpha\beta = \sin\theta\, e^{i\varphi}$ is unchanged when $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are multiplied by the same $e^{i\gamma}$:

$$
x = 2\operatorname{Re}(\bar\alpha\beta), \quad y = 2\operatorname{Im}(\bar\alpha\beta), \quad z = |\alpha|^2 - |\beta|^2 .
$$

The map $S^3 \to S^2$, $(\alpha, \beta) \mapsto (x, y, z)$, is the Hopf fibration; its fibres are the circles of global phase.

| State | $\lvert 0\rangle$ | $\lvert 1\rangle$ | $\lvert +\rangle$ | $\lvert -\rangle$ | $\lvert {+i}\rangle$ | $\lvert {-i}\rangle$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\vec n$ | $(0,0,1)$ | $(0,0,-1)$ | $(1,0,0)$ | $(-1,0,0)$ | $(0,1,0)$ | $(0,-1,0)$ |

<Example>
**Example.** For $|\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\big(|0\rangle + (1+i)|1\rangle\big)$ we have $\bar\alpha\beta = \tfrac{1+i}{3}$, so

$$
\vec n = \big(\tfrac23,\; \tfrac23,\; -\tfrac13\big), \qquad \lVert\vec n\rVert^2 = \tfrac49 + \tfrac49 + \tfrac19 = 1 .
$$

The point lies a little below the equator, at azimuth $\varphi = \pi/4$.
</Example>

### The sphere through the Pauli matrices

The three coordinates are expectation values of the *Pauli matrices*

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
Y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
Z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

in the sense that $x = \langle\psi|X|\psi\rangle$, $y = \langle\psi|Y|\psi\rangle$ and $z = \langle\psi|Z|\psi\rangle$. Each Pauli matrix is Hermitian with trace zero, and they satisfy

$$
\begin{gathered}
X^2 = Y^2 = Z^2 = I, \\[0.5em]
XY = iZ, \quad YZ = iX, \quad ZX = iY, \\[0.5em]
\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_j\sigma_k) = 2\delta_{jk},
\end{gathered}
$$

writing $(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3) = (X, Y, Z)$. Together with $I$ they form a basis of the $2 \times 2$ Hermitian matrices, and the projector onto a state has a simple expansion in that basis:

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi| &= \begin{pmatrix} |\alpha|^2 & \alpha\bar\beta \\ \bar\alpha\beta & |\beta|^2 \end{pmatrix} \\[0.4em]
&= \tfrac12\big(I + xX + yY + zZ\big) = \tfrac12\big(I + \vec n\cdot\vec\sigma\big).
\end{aligned}
$$

Check the entries: the diagonal is $\tfrac12(1 \pm z)$ and the lower-left corner is $\tfrac12(x + iy) = \bar\alpha\beta$.

<Statement>
**Proposition.** If $|\phi\rangle$ and $|\psi\rangle$ have Bloch vectors $\vec m$ and $\vec n$, then

$$
|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2 = \frac{1 + \vec m\cdot\vec n}{2} .
$$
</Statement>

*Proof.* Write $P_\phi = |\phi\rangle\langle\phi|$ and $P_\psi = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$. Then $\operatorname{tr}(P_\phi P_\psi) = \langle\phi|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = |\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2$. Expanding both projectors and using $\operatorname{tr} I = 2$, $\operatorname{tr}\sigma_j = 0$ and $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_j\sigma_k) = 2\delta_{jk}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{tr}(P_\phi P_\psi) &= \tfrac14 \operatorname{tr}\big[(I + \vec m\cdot\vec\sigma)(I + \vec n\cdot\vec\sigma)\big] \\[0.4em]
&= \tfrac14\big(2 + 2\,\vec m\cdot\vec n\big). \qquad\blacksquare
\end{aligned}
$$

The proposition turns statements about $\mathbb{C}^2$ into geometry on the sphere:

- **Orthogonal states are antipodal points**, not perpendicular ones: $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = 0$ exactly when $\vec m\cdot\vec n = -1$. That is the price of the half-angle $\theta/2$. Each orthonormal basis is a diameter of the sphere.
- **A measurement is a choice of axis.** Measuring in the basis with Bloch vectors $\pm\vec m$ gives the outcome $+\vec m$ with probability $\tfrac12(1 + \vec m\cdot\vec n)$, which depends only on the angle between the state and the axis.
- For the standard basis $\vec m = (0,0,1)$, so $P(0) = \tfrac{1+z}{2} = \cos^2\tfrac\theta2$: the height of the point is the measurement statistics.
- The $x$-axis is the $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$ measurement. For the running example $P(+) = \tfrac12\big(1 + \tfrac23\big) = \tfrac56$, as computed earlier.

## Gates

An operation on a qubit must be linear, and it must send unit vectors to unit vectors.

<Statement>
**Proposition.** A linear map $U$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$ satisfies $\lVert U\psi\rVert = \lVert\psi\rVert$ for every $\psi$ if and only if $U^\dagger U = I$. Such matrices are called *unitary*.
</Statement>

*Proof.* We have $\lVert U\psi\rVert^2 = \langle\psi|U^\dagger U|\psi\rangle$, so if $U^\dagger U = I$ the norm is preserved. Conversely, put $A = U^\dagger U - I$. Then $A$ is Hermitian and $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle = 0$ for all $\psi$. Taking $\psi$ to be an eigenvector shows every eigenvalue of $A$ is zero, and a Hermitian matrix with only zero eigenvalues is zero. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Equivalently, the columns of $U$ form an orthonormal basis, and $U$ preserves all inner products: $\langle U\phi|U\psi\rangle = \langle\phi|\psi\rangle$. Every unitary is invertible, with $U^{-1} = U^\dagger$, so quantum gates never lose information; only measurement does. Compare the noisy channel $N_{1/2}$ of the first section, which sends every probability vector to $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ and cannot be undone.

| Gate | Matrix | On the sphere |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $X$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | half turn about the $x$-axis |
| $Y$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | half turn about the $y$-axis |
| $Z$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ | half turn about the $z$-axis |
| $H$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ | half turn about the axis midway between $x$ and $z$ |
| $S$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}$ | quarter turn about the $z$-axis |
| $T$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\pi/4} \end{pmatrix}$ | eighth turn about the $z$-axis |

The Hadamard gate $H$ is the change of basis between $\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle\}$ and $\{|+\rangle, |-\rangle\}$: its columns are $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$, so $H|0\rangle = |+\rangle$, $H|1\rangle = |-\rangle$, and $H^2 = I$ sends them back. Conjugating by $H$ therefore exchanges the roles of the $x$- and $z$-axes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
HZH &= \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \\[0.4em]
&= \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = X .
\end{aligned}
$$

### Every gate is a rotation

The third column of the table is no accident. Let $\hat n$ be a unit vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The relations between the Pauli matrices give $(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)^2 = I$, so the exponential series splits into even and odd powers exactly as it does for $e^{i\theta}$:

$$
R_{\hat n}(\theta) := e^{-i\theta\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma/2} = \cos\tfrac\theta2\, I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma).
$$

<Statement>
**Theorem.** $R_{\hat n}(\theta)$ is unitary with determinant $1$, and it moves the Bloch vector of every state by a rotation through the angle $\theta$ about the axis $\hat n$. Every $2 \times 2$ unitary has the form $e^{i\delta} R_{\hat n}(\theta)$.
</Statement>

We check one case and leave the general statement to the exercises. About the $z$-axis,

$$
\begin{gathered}
R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\theta/2} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\theta/2} \end{pmatrix}, \\[0.6em]
R_z(\theta)\begin{pmatrix} \cos\tfrac{\vartheta}{2} \\ e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\vartheta}{2} \end{pmatrix}
= e^{-i\theta/2}\begin{pmatrix} \cos\tfrac{\vartheta}{2} \\ e^{i(\varphi+\theta)}\sin\tfrac{\vartheta}{2} \end{pmatrix},
\end{gathered}
$$

which, after discarding the global phase, is the same polar angle with azimuth $\varphi + \theta$. The gates in the table are rotations up to a global phase:

$$
\begin{gathered}
X = i\,R_x(\pi), \qquad Z = i\,R_z(\pi), \\[0.5em]
H = i\,R_{\hat h}(\pi) \;\text{ with }\; \hat h = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\hat x + \hat z), \\[0.5em]
S = e^{i\pi/4} R_z\big(\tfrac\pi2\big), \qquad T = e^{i\pi/8} R_z\big(\tfrac\pi4\big).
\end{gathered}
$$

In the language of groups: the matrices $R_{\hat n}(\theta)$ make up $SU(2)$, and the theorem describes a surjective homomorphism $SU(2) \to SO(3)$. It is two-to-one, since $R_{\hat n}(2\pi) = -I$: a full turn of the sphere is the identity rotation but the matrix $-I$, which differs from $I$ by a global phase only. Topologically $SU(2) \cong S^3$ is the double cover of $SO(3)$.

## Interference

The states $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ differ only in a relative phase, and a standard-basis measurement cannot tell them apart: both give $0$ and $1$ with probability $\tfrac12$. A coin flip would do the same.

But apply $H$ before measuring. Since $H|+\rangle = |0\rangle$ and $H|-\rangle = |1\rangle$, the outcome is now certain and distinguishes the two states perfectly. Follow the amplitudes through two Hadamards applied to $|0\rangle$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
|0\rangle \;&\xrightarrow{\;H\;}\; \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|1\rangle \\[0.4em]
&\xrightarrow{\;H\;}\; \tfrac12\big(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\big) + \tfrac12\big(|0\rangle - |1\rangle\big) = |0\rangle .
\end{aligned}
$$

There are two paths to the outcome $1$, through $|0\rangle$ and through $|1\rangle$, with amplitudes $+\tfrac12$ and $-\tfrac12$. They cancel. The two paths to $0$ both have amplitude $+\tfrac12$ and add to $1$.

Now do the same with probabilities. The stochastic matrix for a fair coin flip is

$$
F = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \\ \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad F^2 = F .
$$

Flipping twice is the same as flipping once, because the entries are non-negative and nothing can cancel. Probabilities add; amplitudes can cancel. This is *interference*, and every quantum algorithm in the coming lectures is an arrangement for wrong answers to cancel.

### Dialling the phase

Put a phase gate $P(\varphi) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\varphi} \end{pmatrix}$ between the two Hadamards. Then

$$
\begin{gathered}
H\,P(\varphi)\,H\,|0\rangle = \frac{1 + e^{i\varphi}}{2}\,|0\rangle + \frac{1 - e^{i\varphi}}{2}\,|1\rangle, \\[0.6em]
P(0) = \Big|\frac{1 + e^{i\varphi}}{2}\Big|^2 = \cos^2\frac\varphi2 .
\end{gathered}
$$

The relative phase, invisible to a direct measurement, has been converted into a probability. This three-gate circuit is an interferometer, and it is the seed of phase estimation in lecture 4. The gates $Z$, $S$ and $T$ are $P(\pi)$, $P(\tfrac\pi2)$ and $P(\tfrac\pi4)$.

| Middle gate | $\varphi$ | $P(0) = \cos^2\tfrac\varphi2$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| none | $0$ | $1$ |
| $T$ | $\pi/4$ | $\tfrac12\big(1 + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big) \approx 0.854$ |
| $S$ | $\pi/2$ | $\tfrac12$ |
| $Z$ | $\pi$ | $0$ |
