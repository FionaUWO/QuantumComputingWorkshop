Alice has a qubit $M$ in an unknown state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ and wants Bob to have it. She cannot measure it and send the result, and she cannot copy it. But if she and Bob already share a Bell pair, two classical bits are enough.

1. Alice applies CNOT from $M$ to her half $A$ of the pair, then $H$ to $M$.
2. She measures $M$ and $A$, getting bits $m$ and $a$, and sends them to Bob.
3. Bob applies $X$ to his qubit if $a = 1$, then $Z$ if $m = 1$.

Why it works is a single identity, obtained by expanding and regrouping:

$$
|\psi\rangle_M \otimes |\Phi^+\rangle_{AB} = \tfrac12 \sum_{m, a \in \{0,1\}} |\beta_{ma}\rangle_{MA} \otimes X^a Z^m |\psi\rangle_B,
$$

where the $|\beta_{ma}\rangle$ are the four Bell states. Alice's two gates rotate the Bell basis to the computational basis, so her measurement selects one term, and Bob undoes the leftover $X^a Z^m$.

<Teleport />

Three things are worth noticing. Each of Alice's four outcomes has probability $\tfrac14$ whatever $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are, so her bits reveal nothing about the message. Bob's qubit is useless until the classical bits arrive, so nothing moves faster than light. And Alice's copy is destroyed, so the no-cloning theorem is respected.

<p class="reading">Reading: Nielsen and Chuang, Section 1.3.7.</p>
