A state of the form $|\phi\rangle \otimes |\chi\rangle$ is a *product state*. Counting parameters shows that most states are not of this form: product states need two complex numbers per qubit, while a general state has four.

There is a clean test. Arrange the amplitudes in a matrix

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} a_{00} & a_{01} \\ a_{10} & a_{11} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

For a product state $M$ is the outer product of $(\alpha, \beta)$ and $(\gamma, \delta)$, so it has rank one. Conversely any rank-one matrix factors that way.

<Statement>
**Proposition.** A two-qubit state is a product state if and only if $\det M = a_{00}a_{11} - a_{01}a_{10} = 0$. Otherwise it is *entangled*.
</Statement>

The best known entangled state is the Bell state

$$
|\Phi^+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\big), \qquad \det M = \tfrac12.
$$

Neither qubit of a Bell pair has a state of its own. There is no vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$ that describes $A$ alone.
