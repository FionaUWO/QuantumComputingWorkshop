Factoring reduces to order finding by classical number theory.

<Statement>
**Lemma.** Let $N$ be composite and suppose $y^2 \equiv 1 \pmod N$ with $y \not\equiv \pm 1 \pmod N$. Then $\gcd(y - 1, N)$ and $\gcd(y + 1, N)$ are non-trivial factors of $N$.
</Statement>

*Proof.* $N$ divides $(y-1)(y+1)$ but divides neither factor. So $N$ shares a factor greater than $1$ with each, and neither gcd can be $N$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

If $x$ has even order $r$, then $y = x^{r/2}$ satisfies $y^2 \equiv 1$, and $y \not\equiv 1$ by minimality of $r$. The lemma applies unless $y \equiv -1$.

<Statement>
**Theorem.** Let $N$ be an odd composite number with $m \ge 2$ distinct prime factors, and choose $x$ uniformly among the integers in $[1, N-1]$ coprime to $N$. Then with probability at least $1 - 2^{-m} \ge \tfrac34$, the order $r$ of $x$ is even and $x^{r/2} \not\equiv -1 \pmod N$.
</Statement>

The proof uses the Chinese remainder theorem and the fact that $(\mathbb{Z}/p^a\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic for odd $p$.

<Statement>
**Shor's algorithm.** To factor $N$:

1. If $N$ is even, return $2$. If $N = a^b$ for integers $a \ge 1$, $b \ge 2$, return $a$.
2. Choose $x$ at random in $[2, N-1]$. If $\gcd(x, N) \gt 1$, return it.
3. Find the order $r$ of $x$ modulo $N$ with the quantum order-finding routine.
4. If $r$ is even and $x^{r/2} \not\equiv -1 \pmod N$, compute $\gcd(x^{r/2} \pm 1, N)$ and return a non-trivial factor. Otherwise go back to step 2.
</Statement>

Every step except the third is efficient classically. The total cost is $O(L^3)$ gates, polynomial in the number of digits, whereas the best known classical algorithm, the number field sieve, takes time about $\exp\big(c\,L^{1/3}(\log L)^{2/3}\big)$.

<Example>
$N = 15$, $x = 7$. The powers of $7$ modulo $15$ are $7, 4, 13, 1$, so $r = 4$. Then $x^{r/2} = 49 \equiv 4$, which is not $\pm 1$, and $\gcd(3, 15) = 3$, $\gcd(5, 15) = 5$.
</Example>

### What made it work

Compare with lecture 3. Unstructured search admits only a quadratic speed-up. Here the function $k \mapsto x^k \bmod N$ is periodic, and the Fourier transform over a cyclic group turns a period into a measurable peak. Order finding, discrete logarithms and Simon's problem are all instances of the *hidden subgroup problem* for abelian groups, which this method solves in general. The security of RSA and of elliptic-curve cryptography rests on problems of exactly this type.
