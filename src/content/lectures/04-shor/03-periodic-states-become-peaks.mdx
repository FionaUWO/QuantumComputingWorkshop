Suppose a state is supported on an arithmetic progression. Let $r$ divide $N$, fix an offset $0 \le x_0 \lt r$, and put

$$
|\phi\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{r}{N}} \sum_{m=0}^{N/r - 1} |x_0 + mr\rangle .
$$

<Statement>
**Proposition.**

$$
\mathrm{QFT}_N\,|\phi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt r} \sum_{l=0}^{r-1} e^{2\pi i x_0 l/r}\,\Big|\,l\,\frac{N}{r}\Big\rangle .
$$
</Statement>

*Proof.* The amplitude of $|k\rangle$ is $\tfrac{\sqrt r}{N}\,\omega^{x_0 k} \sum_m \omega^{mrk}$. The sum runs over the $N/r$ powers of $\omega^{rk}$, an $(N/r)$-th root of unity. It equals $N/r$ if $\omega^{rk} = 1$, that is if $k$ is a multiple of $N/r$, and $0$ otherwise. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Two things have happened. The period $r$ of the input has turned into the spacing $N/r$ of the output, as with any Fourier transform. And the offset $x_0$, which a direct measurement of $|\phi\rangle$ would have returned mixed up with a random $m$, now appears only in phases that do not affect the measurement. Measuring gives a uniformly random multiple of $N/r$, whatever $x_0$ was. From $lN/r$ we learn the fraction $l/r$, and from a few such fractions we learn $r$.

When $r$ does not divide $N$ the peaks are not perfectly sharp, but most of the probability still falls on the integers nearest to multiples of $N/r$. Handling that is the job of phase estimation.
