Let $N$ be a positive integer and $x$ an integer coprime to $N$. The *order* of $x$ modulo $N$ is the least $r \ge 1$ with $x^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$, the order of $x$ in the group $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$. No efficient classical algorithm for computing $r$ is known. Write $L = \lceil \log_2 N \rceil$.

Multiplication by $x$ is a permutation of the residues, so

$$
U\,|y\rangle = |xy \bmod N\rangle, \qquad 0 \le y \lt N,
$$

is unitary on $L$ qubits (acting as the identity on $y \ge N$). Its eigenvectors can be written down. For $0 \le s \lt r$ let

$$
|u_s\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt r} \sum_{k=0}^{r-1} e^{-2\pi i sk/r}\,|x^k \bmod N\rangle .
$$

<Statement>
**Proposition.** $U|u_s\rangle = e^{2\pi i s/r}|u_s\rangle$, and $\displaystyle \frac{1}{\sqrt r}\sum_{s=0}^{r-1}|u_s\rangle = |1\rangle$.
</Statement>

*Proof.* $U$ shifts $k$ to $k+1$ in the sum, and $x^r \equiv x^0$, so the sum is reproduced with an extra factor $e^{2\pi i s/r}$. For the second claim, the coefficient of $|x^k\rangle$ in $\sum_s |u_s\rangle$ is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt r}\sum_s e^{-2\pi i sk/r}$, which is $\sqrt r$ for $k = 0$ and $0$ otherwise. $\qquad\blacksquare$

The eigenvalues encode $s/r$, and we do not need to prepare any $|u_s\rangle$: the easy state $|1\rangle$ is an equal superposition of all of them. Phase estimation on $U$ with the second register in $|1\rangle$ returns an approximation to $s/r$ for a uniformly random $s$.

The powers $U^{2^j}$ are multiplications by $x^{2^j} \bmod N$, computed by repeated squaring. The whole modular exponentiation takes $O(L^3)$ gates, and that dominates the cost.

### From a decimal to a fraction

The measurement gives a number $\tilde\varphi$ with $t$ binary digits, close to $s/r$. To extract $r$ we use the continued fraction expansion.

<Statement>
**Theorem.** If $s/r$ is a rational number with $\big|\tfrac{s}{r} - \tilde\varphi\big| \le \tfrac{1}{2r^2}$, then $s/r$ is a convergent of the continued fraction of $\tilde\varphi$.
</Statement>

Since $r \le N$, taking $t = 2L + 1$ digits of accuracy meets the hypothesis. The convergents are computed by the Euclidean algorithm in $O(L^3)$ steps. If $s$ and $r$ happen to share a factor we obtain a divisor of $r$ instead; repeating a few times and taking a least common multiple fixes that.

<Example>
**Example.** Let $N = 15$ and $x = 7$, with $t = 11$ control qubits, so outcomes lie in $0, \dots, 2047$. Suppose the measurement gives $1536$. Then $\tilde\varphi = 1536/2048 = 3/4$, whose continued fraction is $[0; 1, 3]$ with convergents $1$ and $3/4$. The candidate $r = 4$ checks out: $7^4 = 2401 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$.
</Example>
